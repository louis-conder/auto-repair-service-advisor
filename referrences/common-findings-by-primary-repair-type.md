# Common Findings by Primary Repair Type
## Auto Repair Service Advisor

This file maps the most common additional findings discovered
during the most common primary repairs. Organized by what the
customer came in for.

Use this to anticipate what the technician is likely to find
so the communication is prepared before the customer call.
The most professional service advisor is never surprised
by a finding in front of the customer.

---

## Oil Change

**What the technician has access to:**
Full visual inspection of the engine bay, undercarriage
on the lift, and all fluid levels.

**Commonly found:**

- Serpentine belt cracking or glazing
- Coolant hose softness or swelling at the clamps
- Air filter heavily clogged
- Cabin air filter heavily clogged
- Oil leak — valve cover gasket seepage visible from above
- Oil pan gasket seepage visible from below on the lift
- Coolant reservoir low or discolored
- Battery terminals corroded
- Windshield washer fluid low (mention but do not charge)
- Brake fluid dark or past interval
- Power steering fluid dark or low
- Tire tread at or approaching minimum on visual check

**Communication note:**
The oil change is the highest-suspicion visit. Customers
arrive expecting to be told they need something. This means
every finding here requires more careful framing than the
same finding discovered during a larger repair. Lead with
what you can show them. Findings with visible evidence
(dirty air filter, corroded terminals) should be shown
before they are described.

## Brake Service (Pads and/or Rotors)

**What the technician has access to:**
Wheels removed. Full view of caliper, rotor, hub, wheel
bearing, CV axle boot, brake hose condition, and brake
hardware.

**Commonly found:**

- CV axle boot cracked or torn (inner boot especially
  visible with wheel off)
- Wheel bearing — rough feel or play when wheel is in hand
- Brake hose — soft, swollen, or cracked (visible with
  wheel removed)
- Brake hardware — slides, pins, or clips seized or
  corroded, preventing even pad wear
- Caliper — seized piston causing uneven pad wear on
  one side of rotor
- Rotor minimum thickness — may be below spec even if
  pads are not at minimum
- Tire wear pattern visible from inside the wheel well
- Dust shields bent and contacting rotor (brake noise
  source that is not a brake wear issue)
- ABS sensor wire damaged or corroded at the knuckle

**Communication note:**
The wheel-off opportunity is legitimate and should be
stated plainly. "While the wheels were already off"
removes the fishing implication immediately. Customers
accept findings much more readily when they understand
the technician had direct access to that area as part
of the job they authorized.

## Tire Rotation

**What the technician has access to:**
All four wheels removed. Full view of all four corners —
same access as brake service.

**Commonly found:**

- Uneven wear patterns on any tire (outer, inner,
  center, cupping — each tells a different story)
- Tire tread approaching minimum on any corner
- Sidewall cracking or dry rot on older tires
- Wheel bearing noise or play on any corner
- Brake pad thickness visible through the wheel
- CV axle boot condition on all four corners
- Lug nut condition — seized, stripped, or over-torqued
  from previous service

**Communication note:**
Alignment is the most commonly recommended service
following a tire rotation — and the most commonly
suspected upsell. Always show the wear pattern first.
A customer who sees the evidence of uneven wear on
their own tire understands the alignment conversation
before it starts. A customer who is just told they
need an alignment after a rotation feels sold.

## Check Engine Light Diagnosis

**What the technician has access to:**
Full scan tool data, live sensor readings, and
freeze frame data from when the code was set.

**Commonly found:**

- Multiple codes — some related, some coincidental
- Oxygen sensor codes that are symptoms of another
  issue rather than sensor failures
- EVAP system codes from a loose or missing gas cap
  (address this first before any other EVAP repair)
- Misfire codes that point to spark plugs, ignition
  coils, or fuel injectors
- Catalytic converter efficiency codes that are
  caused by long-running misfires
- Mass airflow sensor codes related to a dirty or
  failing air filter
- Thermostat codes — engine not reaching operating
  temperature quickly enough

**Communication note:**
Multiple codes require prioritization and explanation
of relationships before any recommendation is made.
A customer presented with four codes and four prices
will either approve nothing or feel overwhelmed into
approving everything. Neither is the right outcome.
Explain cause and effect. Fix causes before symptoms.
Defer genuinely unrelated codes when resources are
limited.

## AC Service / Recharge

**What the technician has access to:**
Refrigerant levels, system pressure, compressor
operation, condenser and evaporator condition.

**Commonly found:**

- System was low due to a slow leak — recharge without
  finding the leak means the customer will be back.
  Always check for a leak source before recharging.
- Compressor clutch slipping or noisy under load
- Condenser fins damaged or blocked by debris
- Cabin air filter severely clogged (restricts airflow
  through evaporator, reducing cooling efficiency —
  the customer experiences this as weak AC)
- Serpentine belt condition — the AC compressor loads
  the belt significantly

**Communication note:**
If the system is low on refrigerant, the honest
conversation is about why — not just recharging and
sending the customer home. A recharge without finding
the leak source is a temporary fix. The customer
deserves to know that the refrigerant can only go
somewhere, and that somewhere is a leak that should
be found.

## Alignment

**What the technician has access to:**
Full suspension geometry measurements on the alignment
rack. All four corners measured individually.

**Commonly found:**

- Tie rod ends worn — alignment cannot be properly
  set and held with worn tie rods. Setting alignment
  on worn components is a waste of the customer's money.
- Ball joints worn — same issue as tie rods. Suspension
  geometry shifts as worn joints move under load.
- Struts or shocks worn — alignment set on worn struts
  will shift as the suspension compresses differently
  than the rack measured
- Camber bolts missing or seized from previous alignment
  attempts
- Bent control arm from a previous impact the customer
  may not have reported

**Communication note:**
If worn steering or suspension components prevent a
proper alignment, this must be communicated before
the alignment is performed — not after. An alignment
performed on worn components will not hold. Charging
for a service that cannot produce the expected result
without disclosing why is a trust-destroying situation
that is 100% preventable.

## Transmission Service

**What the technician has access to:**
Fluid condition, pan contents (debris indicates internal
wear), filter condition, external solenoids.

**Commonly found:**

- Metal shavings in the pan — indicates internal wear
  beyond normal. This changes the conversation
  significantly.
- Clutch material in the pan — indicates clutch pack
  wear. Common in higher-mileage units.
- External fluid leaks at the pan gasket, cooler lines,
  or axle seals
- Torque converter shudder — felt as a light vibration
  at light throttle between 30-45 mph

**Communication note:**
On high-mileage vehicles, be honest about the risk
of a fluid service disturbing settled debris. Some
high-mileage transmissions have adapted to degraded
fluid and may shift differently after a service.
This is not a reason to withhold the service — it
is information the customer deserves before they
authorize it.

## Cooling System Service

**What the technician has access to:**
Full cooling system — radiator, hoses, water pump,
thermostat, coolant condition, pressure test results.

**Commonly found:**

- Radiator fins clogged with debris, reducing cooling
  capacity
- Water pump weep hole seeping — early sign of pump
  seal failure
- Thermostat housing cracked or leaking at the gasket
- Heater core hoses soft or swollen
- Coolant contaminated with oil — indicates head gasket
  seepage. This changes the scope of the conversation
  entirely and should be escalated to the service
  manager before being presented to the customer.

**Communication note:**
Coolant contaminated with oil is one of the most
important findings to handle carefully. The customer
came in for a cooling system service. They are now
being told about a potential head gasket issue. This
is a significant moment that requires a calm, factual
delivery. Do not speculate about the cost of a head
gasket repair before the technician has confirmed
the diagnosis. State what was found, what it could
indicate, and what the next diagnostic step is.

## Suspension Repair (Struts, Shocks, Control Arms)

**What the technician has access to:**
Full suspension disassembly on the affected corner.
Adjacent components exposed and accessible.

**Commonly found:**

- Strut mount bearing worn — causes clunking or
  groaning on full steering lock after strut replacement.
  Replace at the same time as the strut.
- Coil spring cracked — visible during strut removal.
  A cracked spring with a new strut will fail.
- Control arm bushings — if replacing one, adjacent
  bushings on the same arm are often near the same
  wear point
- Sway bar end links — often disturbed during strut
  removal. Inspect and replace if worn.
- Brake hose — often visible and accessible during
  suspension work at that corner

**Communication note:**
Suspension work often reveals wear in adjacent
components that are inexpensive to replace while
already disassembled and expensive to revisit as
a standalone repair later. Present these clearly
as "while we're already in there" opportunities —
not as additional problems — and be specific about
the labor savings of combining the work.
