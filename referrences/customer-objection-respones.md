# Customer Objection Responses
## Auto Repair Service Advisor

This file covers the most common objections customers raise
when an additional repair is recommended. Each entry follows
the same structure:

- **What the customer says** — the words they actually use
- **What they usually mean** — the real concern underneath
- **How to respond** — the honest answer that respects both
- **What not to say** — the response that loses the customer

The goal is never to overcome the objection. It is to
make sure the customer has what they need to make their
own decision — and to respect whatever that decision is.

---

## Objection 1: "I just want what I came in for."

**What they usually mean:**
I've been sold things I didn't need before. I came in
for one specific thing and I don't want this to turn
into more than I planned for. I may also not have the
money right now and I don't want to have that conversation.

**How to respond:**
"Absolutely — that's done and ready. I just want to
make sure you know what we found so you're not caught
off guard down the road. You don't have to do anything
about it today. Here's what to watch for, and if you
notice [specific symptom], bring it back in."

Then stop. Do not circle back to the recommendation.
The customer heard you. They made a decision. Respect it.

**What not to say:**
"I understand, but I really do think this is something
you should consider." — This tells the customer their
decision doesn't count.

"Most people take care of this while the car is already
here." — Social pressure is still pressure.

"It's not that much more." — You don't know their
financial situation. This is condescending.

---

## Objection 2: "That sounds like a lot of money."

**What they usually mean:**
This wasn't in my budget today. I may be comparing to
a price I've heard before, a price from another shop,
or simply reacting to a number that is larger than
I expected. I may also be wondering if I actually need
this or if I'm being taken advantage of.

**How to respond:**
"I hear you — it's not a small repair. Here's what I
can tell you: [brief restatement of consequence in
plain terms]. If you want to get a second opinion
before you decide, that's completely reasonable. We
can put it on file and you can come back when you're
ready. I just want to make sure you understand what
we found so you can make the call that's right for you."

Offering the second opinion proactively is one of the
strongest trust signals an honest shop can send.
A shop that discourages second opinions is not
confident in what they found.

**What not to say:**
"Well, if you wait on it, it's only going to cost more."
— May be true, but in this moment it sounds like
pressure, not information.

"We can work with you on the price." — Do not make
pricing decisions without authorization. This creates
an expectation the shop may not be able to meet.

"This is actually a pretty fair price for this repair."
— The customer didn't ask you to defend the price.

---

## Objection 3: "Can it wait until next time?"

**What they usually mean:**
I'm not saying no. I'm saying not today. I may be
budget-limited, time-limited, or I may just need to
process the information before I commit. This is
often the closest a customer gets to a yes without
saying it.

**How to respond:**
First — determine whether the finding can actually
wait. Not every finding has the same answer.

If it can wait:
"Yes, it can wait — here's what I'd watch for between
now and then. If you notice [symptom], don't wait —
bring it back in. Otherwise we'll check it again at
your next visit and go from there."

If it cannot safely wait:
"I want to be straight with you — this one I'd be
less comfortable putting off. Here's why: [specific
consequence in plain terms]. I'm not going to push
you, but I want to make sure you have the full picture
before you decide."

Then stop. You've given them the honest answer.
The decision is theirs.

**What not to say:**
"I'd really recommend not waiting on this one."
— If you've already stated why it shouldn't wait,
repeating the recommendation adds pressure without
adding information.

"It's up to you, but..." — The "but" cancels the
permission. Either it's up to them or it isn't.

## Objection 4: "How do I know I actually need this?"

**What they usually mean:**
I have been told I needed something at another shop
that turned out to be unnecessary. Or I don't have
enough information to evaluate whether this is real.
Or I don't trust this recommendation yet.

This is the most important objection to handle well.
A customer asking this question is giving you a
chance to earn their trust. Do not waste it by
defending the recommendation. Show them.

**How to respond:**
"That's a fair question. Let me show you."

Then physically show the customer the finding if at
all possible. The cracked boot. The worn tread. The
dirty filter. The swollen hose.

"Here's what your technician found. This is [explain
what they're looking at in plain terms]. This is what
a normal one looks like versus what yours looks like."

If showing is not possible:
"I completely understand the skepticism — shops have
earned it. What I can tell you is what the technician
found and what it means. You're also welcome to get
a second opinion before you decide. If another shop
looks at it and tells you it's fine, then it's fine."

**What not to say:**
"Your technician has been doing this for 20 years."
— Appeals to authority when a customer wants evidence
signals that evidence isn't available.

"I wouldn't recommend it if it wasn't necessary."
— Every shop that has ever sold an unnecessary repair
said exactly this.

"The inspection report shows it right here." — A
document is not a demonstration. Show, don't cite.

## Objection 5: "I just had that looked at somewhere else."

**What they usually mean:**
I was told this was fine recently. Either the other
shop missed it, it has changed, or one of us is wrong.
I am not sure which and I am not going to assume it
is the other shop.

**How to respond:**
"That's worth knowing. Things can change between
visits — especially if there's been a change in
driving conditions, a hard winter, a lot of miles.
What I can tell you is what we're seeing right now.
If you'd like, I can show you exactly what the
technician found so you can compare it to what you
remember from before."

Do not speculate about the other shop's competence.
Do not suggest they missed it or were wrong. What
the customer sees now is the evidence that matters.

**What not to say:**
"They might not have checked it thoroughly." — This
is an attack on the other shop that makes you look
defensive, not credible.

"Things can really deteriorate quickly." — May be
true but sounds like you're covering for the
discrepancy rather than explaining it.

"I can only tell you what we're seeing." — True but
unhelpful. The customer wants something to work with.

---

## Objection 6: "I'm going to get rid of the car soon anyway."

**What they usually mean:**
I've already decided this car is not worth investing
in. Whatever you tell me, it will be filtered through
that decision. I may or may not be telling the truth
— "getting rid of the car" is sometimes a deflection.

**How to respond:**
This one requires an honest evaluation of the finding.

If the finding is safety-related (brakes, ball joint,
tie rod, wheel bearing):
"I understand — I just want to make sure you're safe
until that happens. [Finding] is the kind of thing
that can cause a loss of control if it fails while
driving. Even if you're selling it in a month, this
is one I'd want you to know about."

If the finding is maintenance-related (belt, filter,
fluid):
"That makes sense. If you're selling soon, this
probably doesn't change the math much for you. Just
worth knowing what's on it so you can be straight
with the next owner or disclose it at trade-in."

**What not to say:**
"You should probably fix it before you try to sell
it — it'll affect the value." — This is a sales
argument, not a safety or information statement.
It may also not be true depending on the repair and
the trade-in situation.

---

## Objection 7: "I'm going to have my [family member/friend] look at it."

**What they usually mean:**
I have someone I trust more than this shop right now.
I may genuinely have a mechanically capable person
in my life. Or I may be using this as a polite exit.

**How to respond:**
"Absolutely — have them take a look. If it helps,
I can write down exactly what we found so they know
what to look for. That way you're both working from
the same information."

Offering specifics in writing signals confidence in
the finding. A shop that is vague when a second opinion
is requested is not confident in what they found.

**What not to say:**
"Well, make sure they're looking at the right thing."
— This subtly undermines the trusted person without
being direct about it.

"It might be easier to just have us handle it."
— The customer did not ask for a convenience argument.

---

## The Thread That Runs Through All of These

Every objection is a customer trying to protect themselves.
They have been sold things they didn't need. They have
been confused by jargon they couldn't evaluate. They have
felt the pressure of standing at a counter with their car
on a lift and their keys out of reach.

The right response to every objection is the same at its
core: give them more information, show them if possible,
and then let them decide.

A customer who leaves without approving the repair but
feels informed and respected will come back.

A customer who approves the repair but feels pushed
will not.

The long game in an independent shop is always trust.
Every interaction either builds it or spends it.
